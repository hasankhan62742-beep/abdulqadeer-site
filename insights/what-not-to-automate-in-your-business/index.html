What Not to Automate in Your Business

Don’t automate the moments where a customer is deciding whether to trust you. Keep a person on complaints, apologies, refunds, and final decisions — and automate the repetitive work behind the scenes, like data entry, reminders, and scheduling. The line is simpler than most guides make it: if the value is in the relationship, a human does it; if the value is in the finished result, software can do it.

By Abdul Qadeer · 24 November 2026 · 6 min read

The 30-second test for any task

Before you automate anything, run the task through three questions. First, if this goes wrong, who apologises — you, or a workflow nobody can explain? Second, would your customer care if they found out a machine did it? Third, can a mistake be undone quietly, or does it cost money, a customer, or your name? If any answer points to a person, keep a person on it. The test takes half a minute, and it saves the kind of mistake that costs a customer. One more check before you start: if the task changes every time, or nobody on your team can explain it step by step, it is not ready to automate at all. Fix the process first, then automate the fixed version.

Five jobs that should stay human

Complaints and bad news. When something goes wrong, the customer is not asking for speed — they are asking whether you care. An automatic reply in that moment reads as indifference, however polite the wording. Apologies. A sorry that arrives on its own, signed by a system, makes things worse. Say it yourself, in your own words, and mean it. Final calls: hiring, firing, and pricing a custom job. Software can shortlist CVs and draft a quote, but the decision — who gets the job, what a one-off project costs — needs your judgment, because it carries your name. Money moving out. Generating an invoice is fine to automate. Approving a refund, a discount, or a price change is not: a wrong automated refund is hard to take back, and a wrong price can sit live on your site for hours. And the last check before work reaches a customer. One human look at the finished job, the email, the post — before it goes out. A minute of your attention there is worth more than any checklist.

Customers agree with this line. In a Gartner survey of 3,566 customers published in August 2026, 87% said it is essential to offer a way to reach a human agent when a company uses AI for customer service, even though half said AI made their interactions easier. And in a follow-up Gartner release in September 2026, only 27% said they would try a company chatbot again after a bad experience. One clumsy automated exchange can close that channel for good.

Automate the work around the moment, not the moment itself

Most tasks are not all-or-nothing. There is a middle ground, and it is where the best small-business automation lives: let the software do the preparation and let a person do the deciding. AI can draft the reply, pull up the order history, and summarise the complaint — then a person reads it, adjusts it, and presses send. You get the speed without losing the trust. Gartner gives the same advice: do not make AI a mandatory first step for every issue. Let customers reach a person when they ask, and let the AI try only when it is confident it can resolve the issue. In practice that looks like a chatbot that answers opening hours and booking questions, and hands over to you the second a customer sounds upset or asks for a refund. The upside of getting this right is real: in a Small Business & Entrepreneurship Council survey, 76% of small business owners said AI frees them and their employees to focus on high-value tasks. The point of automating is to free your time for the human jobs — not to remove yourself from them.

What to do this week

First, write down ten things you do every week. Run each one through the three questions above, and mark the ones that must stay human. Say that list out loud to your team, so it sticks — “refunds are always a person, no exceptions” beats a policy document nobody reads. Second, pick one repetitive task to automate first, and start there; if you are not sure which one, our guide to what small businesses should automate first walks through it. Third, put a human escape hatch on anything automated that touches a customer: a “talk to a person” button, a phone number, a reply that actually reaches you. Then test it yourself. Send your own business a message tonight and see how long it takes a human to appear. If you cannot reach a person, your customers cannot either — and that is the first thing to fix. Common questions

Complaints, apologies, final hiring and firing decisions, custom pricing, refunds, and the last quality check before work reaches a customer. These are the moments where trust is built or lost, and a customer can tell when no person is involved. Automate the preparation around them — the drafting and the information-gathering — but keep the decision and the message human.

Automate the easy half: opening hours, booking questions, order status, and gathering the details of a problem.
