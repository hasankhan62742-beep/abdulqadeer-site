How to Build a Small Business Dashboard

A small business dashboard is one screen that shows the handful of numbers that tell you whether your business is healthy — cash in the bank, new leads, jobs booked, and money customers still owe you. You build one by choosing five to ten numbers that answer the questions you already ask every morning, giving each number a source and a target, and putting them somewhere you will actually look. Start in a spreadsheet; you can automate it later.

By Abdul Qadeer · 23 November 2026 · 6 min read

Start with the questions, not the charts

Most dashboards are ignored for a reason that has nothing to do with charts. That reason is set before the first chart is drawn: the builder started from the data that was available, not from the decision the screen had to support. The dashboard guides that small businesses actually stick with all say the same thing: start from the questions you ask every morning. Are we on pace to hit this month's revenue target? How many leads are still waiting for a reply? Is there enough cash to cover next week's bills? Write down the two or three questions that matter most to you. Every number on your dashboard has to answer one of them. If a number doesn't answer a question you ask, it doesn't belong on the screen. This one step is what separates a dashboard you check every morning from a wall of charts you stop opening after a week.

Pick five to ten numbers that earn their place

Most guides land on the same range: five to ten metrics. Fewer and you are guessing; more and you stop looking. Spread them across four areas so no part of the business hides. Money in: today's revenue against target, and cash on hand. Money owed: invoices past due, and how much is stuck there. Work coming in: new enquiries in the last 24 hours, unanswered leads, and jobs or orders booked. Customers: how many come back. Pick the numbers that fit your kind of business — a salon watches bookings and no-shows, a shop watches sales and stock, a contractor watches quotes sent and quotes won. For each number, write down three things: where it comes from, what "good" looks like, and what you will do when it goes bad. If a number can't tell you what to do next, it is taking up space that a useful number could have.

Give every number a home and a refresh rhythm

A dashboard doesn't create numbers; it collects them. Your accounting software knows the money, your booking system knows the jobs, your inbox or customer list knows the leads, and your bank knows the cash. For each number on your list, name the one place it comes from. If two systems disagree about the same number, pick one as the truth and stick to it. Then decide how often each number needs refreshing. Cash, sales and unanswered leads change daily, so they get checked daily. Pipeline and close rate make sense weekly. Margins and customer costs only move month to month, so check them monthly. A number that takes an hour to update will quietly stop being updated — so if a number can't be refreshed in seconds, simplify how you measure it.

Lay it out so the worst number finds you

One screen, nothing more. Put the two numbers that decide your day at the top: cash on hand and today's revenue against target. Underneath, group the rest by what they are: leads on one side, work and money owed on the other. Give every number a target and a colour — green when it is on track, yellow when it needs watching, red when it needs action today. You should be able to read the whole screen in under a minute and know exactly where the problem is. If you have to scroll, hunt, or do maths in your head, the layout is wrong. The test is simple: hand it to someone who doesn't run the business and ask them what is going wrong. If they can point to it, the dashboard works.

Keep it alive with a five-minute morning habit

A dashboard that nobody opens is just a report. The habit that keeps it alive takes five minutes. Every morning, open it, find the worst number, and do one thing about it: call the customer who hasn't paid, follow up the lead that went quiet, or move a job to fill an empty slot. Fix the worst number first, and only move on when its trend turns around. Once a month, look at the whole screen and delete any number nobody acted on — if a month passed and nobody used it, it doesn't belong there. Start in a spreadsheet. Google Sheets costs nothing, and Google's Looker Studio is free and connects to Sheets and Google Analytics when you are ready for automatic updates. A dashboard built this way grows with you instead of gathering dust.
