Why Don't Customers Reply to My Quotes?

You sent the quote on Tuesday. It's Friday, your inbox is quiet, and you've checked your sent folder twice just to make sure it actually went out. It did — so now what? Send another email and risk sounding desperate, or let it go and watch the job go to someone else?

By Abdul Qadeer · 17 November 2026 · 7 min read

Silence usually isn't a no

Here's the part most owners get wrong: a quiet customer is rarely a customer who said no. More often, they're a customer who got busy. The quote landed while they were dealing with something else, and by the time they had a free minute it was buried under fifty newer emails. Or they're waiting on a partner to weigh in, or comparing three quotes — yours may be the one they liked best, and they just haven't got around to telling you yet.

That matters, because the way you read the silence decides what you do next. Assume they rejected you and you stop — the job goes to whoever followed up. Assume they're busy and you follow up once, properly, giving them an easy way back in.

What the research actually says

This isn't just a feeling — the numbers back it up. In a 2011 Harvard Business Review audit of 2,241 US companies, the average reply to a web inquiry took 42 hours, and 23% of companies never replied at all. Firms that reached a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to have a real conversation with the decision-maker than firms that waited even an hour longer — and more than 60 times as likely as firms that waited a day or more.

The follow-up side is just as clear. An analysis by Belkins of 16.5 million emails found reply rates highest on the first email and falling with every message after it — and four or more emails in a sequence more than triples spam complaints and unsubscribes. Velocify found that 93% of leads that eventually converted were reached by the sixth call attempt — yet around half of leads were only ever called once.

One more number: research compiled by Invesp and cited by HubSpot found that 48% of salespeople never follow up at all, and a further 44% give up after a single attempt. Follow up three or four times, properly, and you're already doing more than almost everyone else in your market.

A follow-up rhythm that doesn't feel pushy

Stop thinking of follow-up as chasing, and start thinking of it as being useful four times. Here's a rhythm that works for most service businesses — four touches over about two weeks, each one with a reason to exist.

Touch 1 — the day after you send the quote. Keep it short: “Just checking the quote came through OK — happy to walk through anything on it.” You're not selling — you're making sure the email arrived and the door is open.

Touch 2 — three or four days later. Add something new: an answer to a question they might have, a date you could start, a detail that makes the decision easier. Give them a reason to reply, not just a reminder you exist.

Touch 3 — about a week in. Change the channel: if you've been emailing, try a quick call or a text. Some people never answer email properly but will happily talk for two minutes.

Touch 4 — around day fourteen. Close the loop, kindly: “I'll assume the timing isn't right for now — no hard feelings at all. If anything changes, I'm here.” This one gets more replies than you'd expect, because it takes the pressure off and sounds like a person, not a sales machine.

What to say instead of “just checking in”

“Just checking in” is the one line every customer has learned to ignore. It says nothing, asks nothing, and gives them nothing to reply to. Compare it with: “Quick question — is the start date the thing holding this up, or is it the price? Either way I can work with it.” Or: “I can hold your slot until Thursday if that helps — after that I'll have to release it.” That's honest, specific, and useful.

The rule: every message should answer a question they might have, remove a worry, or make the next step smaller. If yours does none of those three things, rewrite it until it does.

Automate the remembering, not the relationship

Here's where most small businesses actually lose the quote: not in the writing, but in the remembering. You're on a job, the phone rings, the day runs away — and the follow-up you meant to send on Thursday quietly becomes never. That's the part worth automating: a simple system can watch for quotes with no reply, remind you when it's time to follow up, and draft the message for you to approve. The timing gets handled; the words stay yours.

That distinction matters. Customers can tell the difference between a business that follows up because a system reminded them and one that follows up because a robot wrote the email. The first feels organized; the second feels cold. Use automation for the part you're bad at — remembering — and keep the human part for yourself.
