What Should I Automate Before Hiring My Next Employee?

Before hiring, automate the repeat jobs first: answering the same questions, chasing follow-ups, and copying details between apps. A system that runs while you are busy is cheaper than a hire — and it shows you what work actually needs a person. You're swamped. The inbox never empties, the phone rings while you're on a job, and the obvious answer feels like hiring someone. But a hire is the most expensive way to fix a workload problem — and half the time, the work doesn't need a person. It needs a system that runs while you're busy.

By Abdul Qadeer · 18 November 2026 · 7 min read

Hiring costs more than the salary

According to the Society for Human Resource Management's 2025 benchmarking survey, the average cost of hiring one person for a non-executive role is $5,475 — and that covers recruiting spend alone, before a single dollar of salary, training, or equipment. Then the new person needs weeks before they're useful, and if it doesn't work out, you pay again. Hiring should be the second step, not the first. Before you post the job, check how much of the overload is work that only a person can do — and how much is the same small tasks, repeated every day, that a system could quietly handle instead.

First, find where the hours actually go

Most owners are surprised. A 2023 survey by Time etc of 251 US entrepreneurs found owners spent 36% of a 45.5-hour working week on small administrative tasks — roughly 16 hours a week. The jobs eating that time aren't dramatic: logging expenses, managing schedules, creating invoices, entering data, and chasing late payers. None of them needs a new employee. All of them repeat. Spend one normal week jotting down every admin task. The five that show up most often are your automation list — and they're usually the reason you're thinking about hiring in the first place.

Three tests before you automate anything

Not every task should be handed to software. Run each one through three quick tests. Does it repeat — same steps, several times a week? Does it need almost no judgment? And if it goes wrong once, is the damage small and fixable? A task that passes all three is a safe first automation. Pricing calls, unhappy customers, and anything where a wrong answer could lose a client all fail the third test. Let software handle everything around those moments — the reminders, the filing, the follow-up — but keep the moment itself with a person.

The five things to automate first

1. Answering and logging every inquiry. Every form fill, email, and missed call should land in one list automatically, with an instant acknowledgement to the customer. Nothing waits for you to notice it, and nothing gets lost while you're out on a job. If replies are already slipping, start here: our guide on how to stop losing inquiries to slow replies walks through the setup.

2. Booking and reminders. Online booking that checks your real availability, a confirmation the moment someone books, and a reminder the day before with a reschedule link. It cuts the back-and-forth and the no-shows.

3. Invoices and payment reminders. Send the invoice the moment a job is done, attach a payment link, and schedule two polite reminders — one before the due date and one after. Chasing late payers is the admin job owners resent most, and it's almost entirely mechanical. The full setup is in our guide on getting customers to pay on time.

4. Moving data between your apps. Sage's September 2026 research of 1,500 small businesses found owners spend an average of 2.9 hours a week re-typing information between systems that don't talk to each other. Connect the handoffs you already do by hand — an inquiry creates a contact, a booking updates the record, a finished job creates a draft invoice — and the copying stops.

5. Your weekly numbers. Counting leads, replies, quotes, and wins by hand every Friday is exactly the kind of job to hand off. A short Monday summary — leads in, quotes out, invoices open — beats an hour of spreadsheet work.

What to keep human

Automation works best when it does the sorting, logging, counting, and drafting — and you keep the deciding. A complaint deserves a person. A price for a complicated job deserves a person. The rule is simple: if a wrong answer would cost you a customer or your reputation, a human sends it. Everything around that — the reminders, the filing, the follow-up nudges — can run on its own. If you'd like help setting that up, our business automation services start with exactly this kind of review.

Then decide if you still need the hire

Give the automations a month, then look again. Often the job you were about to hire for has shrunk to a few hours — and what you actually need is a part-timer, or someone for the skilled work only. Instead of paying someone to chase invoices and copy details between apps, you hire for the work that grows the business. A 2026 survey by U.S. Bank of 1,000 small business owners found 44% already use AI for process automation — this is normal now, not early. One honest note: the UK Government's 2025 survey of AI-using businesses (published January 2026) found that while 56% reported higher productivity, 77% reported no change in revenue. Automation buys you time and capacity first. Revenue may follow, but it's slower — so start for the hours, not for a sales promise. More practical guides live in our Insights.

Start with one task this week

Don't automate everything at once. Pick the task you did most, run it through the three tests, and set up that one workflow. When it runs without you for a week, pick the next. Four or five tasks in, your week looks different — and the hiring decision gets clearer.
