How to Get Customers to Leave Reviews

The simplest way to get customers to leave reviews is to ask them — right after a good experience, with a direct link, and without making it a chore. According to BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey, 83% of people who were asked to leave a review went on to leave one. Most owners never ask, though. They wait, they hope, and they wonder why the business down the road has hundreds of reviews while they have a handful.

By Abdul Qadeer · 21 November 2026 · 7 min read

Your reviews are the front door now

Before a new customer calls you, they check you. According to BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey of 1,002 US adults, 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses — and 41% say they always read them before choosing. That “always” number jumped from 29% last year. People are checking more, not less. And they judge fast: 47% of consumers won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews, and 74% only care about reviews written in the last three months. A profile whose newest review is from last year reads like a business that has gone quiet — and a stranger scrolling past has no reason to stick around and find out otherwise. Takeaway: reviews aren't a nice extra. They're the first thing a new customer sees — and the reason they call you, or the next name on the list.

Ask when they're happiest, not when it's convenient

Timing is the whole game. Ask a happy customer the same day the work is done, while they're still pleased and before life moves on. According to the same BrightLocal survey, customers are more than twice as likely to write about a good experience (60%) as a bad one (29%). The goodwill is there. It just has an expiry date. Wait a week and the moment is gone — the customer meant to do it and forgot. Ask in person, right there at the counter or as you wrap up the job: “Would you mind leaving us a quick review? It takes about a minute.” A real person asking beats an email nobody opens. Takeaway: build the ask into the end of every job, the same way you build in the invoice.

Make leaving a review a one-tap job

Every extra step costs you reviews. If a customer has to search for your business, find the review button, and work out where to click, most of them won't bother. Send them straight there. Get your direct Google review link from your Google Business Profile — it's free — and shorten it so it's easy to share. Print it as a QR code on the receipt, the counter card, or the thank-you note. Put the link in your follow-up text or email. The rule is simple: one tap from the ask to the review box. If it takes longer than a minute, you've lost them. Takeaway: the shorter the path, the more reviews you get.

Automate the ask so it happens every single time

Asking in person works, but only when someone remembers. The businesses with hundreds of reviews don't rely on memory — they have a system. A short text or email goes out after every job, automatically, with the review link inside. One gentle follow-up if they didn't respond. That's it. This is the kind of small, repeatable job that business automation handles well: the message sends itself, the same day, every time, and you never have to chase it. If you want the wording right, our guide on how to text customers covers what to say and when to send it. Takeaway: if the ask depends on someone remembering, it won't happen. Put it on rails.

Reply to every review — yes, the bad ones too

Reviews aren't a one-way street. According to BrightLocal, 89% of consumers expect a business to respond to reviews, and 80% say they're more likely to use a business that replies to all of them. A reply shows the next reader there's a real person behind the business. Keep it short and specific — mention what they came in for, thank them by name if they gave one. And don't paste the same line under every review: half of consumers say generic, templated replies put them off. For a bad review, stay calm, say sorry for the experience, and move the conversation offline: “I'm sorry this happened — please call me so I can make it right.” Future customers read your reply more than the complaint. Takeaway: your replies are marketing. Write them like the next customer is watching — because they are.

The line you don't cross

Never pay for a review, and never offer a discount or a freebie in exchange for a good one. In 2024 the FTC made buying and selling fake reviews illegal in the US, and offering incentives breaks the rules of Google and Yelp — your listing can be penalized or removed. It backfires with customers too: in BrightLocal’s 2026 survey, 97% of consumers think businesses caught using fake reviews should face punishment. Ask for honest feedback instead. One real four-star review from a real customer is worth more than ten suspicious five-star ones. Takeaway: ask for honesty, not praise. The honest reviews are the ones people trust.

Your next step, this week

Start small. Count your reviews on Google today, and check the date of your newest one. If you're under 20, that's your first target. If your newest review is months old, freshness is the gap, not the total. Set up your direct review link, print the QR code, and ask your next five happy customers in person. Then automate the ask so it keeps happening on your busiest days. Reviews compound: each one makes the next customer a little more likely to choose you.
