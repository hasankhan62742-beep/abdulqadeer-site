What I need from you to start

Automation starts with watching how the work happens today, so the first thing I need is a walkthrough of the process you want to automate — who does it, what tools they use, and where it slows down or goes wrong. You do not need to document anything in advance; the process mapping at the start of the project is where we work it out together, and the most expensive repetitive step is the one automated first.

I also need access to the accounts involved — your forms, spreadsheets, CRM, email and WhatsApp business channel — or an introduction to the person who manages them. And I need to know what should happen when something fails: who gets told, and what the fallback is. Every workflow is designed with its failure path agreed in writing before it is built, so a failed step never silently disappears.

If you are not sure which process to automate first, start with the one your team complains about most. The task that eats an hour a day, the follow-up that depends on someone's memory, the report that is always late. Those are usually the processes where automation pays back fastest — and they are also the easiest to describe, because your team already knows exactly what is wrong with them.

What happens after handoff

When a workflow goes live, you receive the automation running on your real accounts and data, error alerts switched on, and documentation describing what each flow does, what triggers it, and what happens when something fails. Your team keeps working in the same tools — the automation runs behind them, moving data, sending messages and filing reports on schedule.

After that, the workflows keep running without daily attention. When a tool changes, a process changes, or you want a new step automated, that is a new piece of work, scoped in writing. The documentation is written so the flows can be understood and changed later, and your data and accounts stay yours throughout: nothing is locked away, and nothing depends on me being available for the work to keep running.

One thing worth knowing: automation makes a bad process faster, not better. If a step is broken — a form nobody fills in properly, a handoff nobody owns — the process mapping is where we find it and fix it first. Automating a broken process only breaks it more quickly, so the honest work sometimes starts before the automation does.
