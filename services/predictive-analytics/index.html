What I need from you to start

A forecast starts with your history, so the first thing I need is access to the records behind the question — customer history for a churn forecast, sales history for demand, case records for risk. What matters most is that outcomes are recorded: who left, what sold, when. Several months to a few years of history is the usual range, but the data audit at the start of the project answers this for your specific case — and if the data cannot support a reliable forecast, I will tell you before you spend anything.

I also need one person who understands the decision the forecast must serve — usually the person who makes it. A short conversation about what the forecast should change, and what a wrong forecast would cost, shapes the whole project. With the history and that conversation, the forecast is built for a real decision, not for a report nobody reads.

Your history does not need to be tidy to be useful. Real business records have gaps, duplicates and changing definitions — that is normal, and cleaning them is part of the work. What matters is that the records exist and the outcomes in them are real. A forecast built on invented or guessed history would be worthless, so the audit checks the real thing first.

What happens after handoff

When the forecast goes live, you receive the dashboard or connection your team will use, the test report showing how it performed on information it had never seen, and documentation explaining what it does, where it is weak, and what information would improve it. Your team sees the forecast and the reasons behind it, so they can trust — and challenge — the result.

A forecast is not finished when it goes live. As new information arrives, it is kept up to date and watched, so problems are caught early and the forecast stays honest. When you want it to predict something new — another product line, another kind of risk — that is a new piece of work, scoped in writing. The forecast and its data stay yours throughout: nothing is locked away, and the documentation means the work can be picked up and continued by anyone.

The most important habit after handoff is comparing the forecast with what actually happened. That comparison is how you know whether to keep trusting it, and it is also how it gets better: the gap between forecast and reality is the most useful information the system produces.
